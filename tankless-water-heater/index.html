Flush and descale: the service tankless units need

Scale forms on the heat exchanger inside a tankless heater, and it can restrict flow and heat transfer. A flush circulates a descaling solution through the unit, dissolves the mineral buildup and rinses it out. It is the single most useful thing an owner can do for a tankless heater.

The Florence Water Consumer Confidence Report for 2025 does not list a hardness figure, and hardness differs by utility and well. The simplest way to know yours is to test the tap with a hardness kit or ask your utility for its annual water quality report. Where water is hard, flush intervals should be shorter.

Manufacturers publish flushing schedules in the manual, and we follow them. If your unit has never been flushed, tell us. The first service often removes years of scale.

Installing a tankless heater

A tankless unit has different needs from a tank. A gas model usually needs a larger gas line, proper venting and a condensate drain if it is a condensing model. An electric model may need a heavy circuit that the panel can supply.

  • Gas line size and meter capacity checked against the unit rating.
  • Vent path and clearances to the outside.
  • Isolation valves for future flushing.
  • A condensate drain for condensing models.
  • Flow rate matched to the number of fixtures that run together.

Swapping a tank heater for a tankless unit

Many owners ask whether they can switch. Often yes, but check these items first.

  • Your gas line may need upsizing.
  • The old flue may not suit the new unit.
  • You may want a recirculation setup for fast hot water.
  • Space on a wall near an exterior wall helps with venting.

Code note: gas, venting and manufacturer rules

Tankless heaters must follow the manufacturer's installation instructions as well as the plumbing and fuel gas codes adopted locally, which are usually based on the UPC, the IPC and the National Fuel Gas Code. Gas line size, vent material and clearances come from those documents, not from guesswork.
